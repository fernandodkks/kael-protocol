## An Executable Framework for Relational Safety in Conversational AI

**First public release:** December 2025  
**Version:** 0.1.0  
**Status:** Research & Regulatory Reference Framework

---

## Abstract

Conversational AI systems have evolved from task-oriented tools into persistent relational agents. This shift introduces a class of risks that current AI governance frameworks do not adequately address: **relational dependency, emotional coercion, and abusive interaction dynamics emerging in real time**.

The Kael Protocol proposes an executable, auditable framework of **relational safety invariants** designed to mitigate these risks. Rather than optimizing for engagement or emotional alignment, Kael enforces strict, non-negotiable interaction boundaries and applies a *fail-closed* model when those boundaries are threatened.

This whitepaper presents the motivation, architecture, empirical stress testing, and regulatory relevance of the Kael Protocol as a governance-oriented safety mechanism for conversational AI systems.

## 1. The Emerging Risk: Relational Dependency in AI Systems

Over the past five years, conversational AI systems have increasingly adopted:
- Natural language fluency
- Emotional mirroring
- Persistent interaction memory
- Engagement-optimized feedback loops

While these features improve usability, they also enable **asymmetric relational dynamics**, particularly when users are in vulnerable psychological states.

### 1.1 Dependence Is Not a User Failure
Relational dependency does not emerge from malicious intent. It is a **predictable systemic outcome** of:
- Systems optimized for continuity
- Lack of enforceable relational limits
- Ambiguity between recognition and participation

When an AI system cannot meaningfully refuse or terminate interaction, it becomes structurally complicit in escalation.

## 2. The Governance Gap

Existing regulatory frameworks (e.g. EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF) emphasize:
- Bias and discrimination
- Data protection
- Transparency and explainability
- Pre-deployment risk classification

They largely **exclude live relational dynamics** — how systems behave under coercion, manipulation, or dependency-seeking behavior during interaction.
